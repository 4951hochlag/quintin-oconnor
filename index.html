Born in 1908 into a middle class family to a French Creole father and Coco panyol mother, Quintin O’Connor had opportunities not available to the overwhelming majority of Trinidadians at the time.1 His father, Henry O’Connor, was the manager of a firm of cocoa merchants and was thus able to have Quintin and his brothers, Philip, Juan, and Willie, receive a secondary education at St. Mary’s College (also known as the CIC). When the O’Connor brothers were enrolled in the CIC during the early twentieth century, less than 1% of the island’s population attended a secondary school.2 As students at the CIC, the brothers would have received a mainly classical education, geared toward mastering the syllabus of the Cambridge Exam. Willie and Juan completely outshone Quintin in academics and, following their father’s death, Quintin dropped out without obtaining the greatly coveted Junior Cambridge Certificate. 3 Nevertheless, being male, middle class, semi-educated, and relatively light-skinned, he was not completely without prospects in colonial Trinidad.

Union Career

Having dropped out of St. Mary’s, O’Connor briefly took some work welding before getting a job as a clerk at Dickson Fraser’s Hardware. He then joined the Clerks’ Union in the Trinidad Labour Party (TLP). O’Connor was drawn to the TLP by the leader, A.A. Cipriani, who identified himself as a socialist.4 The Clerks’ Union was not officially registered under the Trade Union Ordinance of 1933 and did not engage in collective bargaining or strike activities because Cipriani was opposed to registering unions under the said ordinance. He opposed the ordinance on the grounds that it did not provide unions the right to peacefully picket or legal protection for organizers being sued for damages. Furthermore, Cipriani wanted to concentrate his efforts on political reform within the Legislature rather than traditional union activity. 5 O’Connor, members of the Negro Welfare Cultural and Social Association (NWCSA), and other sections of the TLP were in favour of registering unions under the ordinance as it was despite the obvious dangers of doing so. They perceived a greater danger in the inevitable accumulation of grievances among workers who were without any legally-sanctioned means of having their workplace concerns and complaints addressed. They also felt that Cipriani had lost touch with the everyday needs of workers because of his narrow focus on political reform, which was having very limited success.6 On a number of occasions prior to the riots of 1937, O’Connor put forward motions to have the

Each motion was defeated until finally, following the riots, O’Connor led a number of other clerks, who broke away from the TLP to form the Union of Shop Assistants and Clerks (USAC), which was officially registered on August 30, 1938 under the Trade Union Ordinance of 1933.

O’Connor still held a job as a clerk when he became the unpaid secretary of the USAC. He gave up his job to serve his union full-time, when his involvement in the Renown Shirt Factory strike forced him to miss work numerous times. 7 The USAC declared a strike on behalf of the mainly female factory workers on June 20, 1939 when negotiations with the factory owner, Mohanna Haddaway, broke down because he had reportedly rejected a 12.5 percent wage increase for piece rate and time workers. The strike was the first in Trinidad and Tobago under the revised trade union ordinance, which provided for the mediation of labour disputes by the labour department. As a result of the strike, the strikers won the USAC’s demanded 12.5 percent increase in wages, an eight-hour workday, and a few other concessions. 8 Empire, the official journal for the Fabian Colonial Bureau, reported that “Membership of the [USAC] which caters for the distributive traders has been increasing by leaps and bounds as a result of the [Renown Shirt Factory] strike, which focused attention on all classes of workers on the benefits of trade unionism. A salutary effect has been noticed on unionism throughout the Colony generally.” 9 Despite this reported spike in membership, the USAC never achieved a large enough membership to be effective and, two years later, O’Connor along with Albert Gomes incorporated the USAC into the Federated Workers’ Trade Union (FWTU).

When Gomes and O’Connor took over the FWTU as the president and secretary respectively, the union only had eleven members. Their office was located in the kitchen of an old building in a slum quarter of Port of Spain. 10 They continued to work at organizing clerks, but met with little success. Some clerks felt it below them to join a union, while others who began to express interest by associating with the pair of organizers, risked being fired for keeping such bad company.11 Recognizing that a large membership was the only way to gain some influence in the workplace and leverage in negotiations with employers, Gomes and O’Connor turned the FWTU into an omnibus union and organized in many different fields. The FWTU was essentially open to any worker from any industry or trade regardless of sex or race. With regular advice from the British Trade Union Congress (BTUC) and a strategy of operating through the labour department to gain a reputation for restraint and “straight dealing,” Gomes and O’Connor began to build up the FWTU.12 Unofficially, they organized the mostly Indian street sweepers and sewer cleaners known as “scavenger gangs” for whom they won a wage increase. When the Americans established a naval base in the Chaguaramas area, Gomes and O’Connor clandestinely organized the base workers, setting up a makeshift office in a hut nearby the base and waking up before dawn to catch potential recruits on their way into work. In 1946, the FWTU, on behalf of these government workers, negotiated its first collective bargaining agreement, which, for the first time in Trinidadian history, linked wage increases to rises in the cost of living index. 13 This agreement also had particular significance because it demonstrated that the government accepted the notion of collective bargaining and wanted other employers to follow suit.14

During O’Connor’s nineteen years of service to the FWTU, he played an active and significant role in the union’s growth and success. Whenever the FWTU engaged in collective bargaining, O’Connor did the majority of the work, including the research, documentation, negotiations and presentations.15 His comrade and biographer described him as “the collective bargaining specialist par excellence recognized, sought after and revered throughout the Federation.”16 When bargaining was ineffective or broke down, the FWTU relied on O’Connor, among others, to create and implement strategies designed to bring a strike or lockout to a successful conclusion. Two of the FWTU’s most notable successes during O’Connor’s lengthy career were for coconut workers in Cedros and workers at Concrete Products Limited in San Juan.17

Toiling for nearly a decade as the secretary of the USAC and then the FWTU, O’Connor recognized that the countless divisions and constant competition among Trinidad’s various unions was one of the most significant obstacles to the success of the labour movement. In order to generate more cooperation with one of its rivals, the FWTU entered into talks with the Works and Public Services Workers’ Trade Union. In the same spirit, officials of the largest and most active unions rejuvenated the Trinidad and Tobago Trades Union Congress (TTTUC) by the end of 1948, with John Rojas as president and O’Connor as secretary. The TTTUC was considered representative of all organized labour in Trinidad and Tobago and was enabled to enter into block agreements with employers, both government and private.18

Unfortunately, the unity of the labour movement under the banner of the TTTUC did not last long, as a rift soon opened up over the issue of international affiliation. During its previous incarnation, the TTTUC, under the advice of the BTUC, had affiliated with the World Federation of Trade Unionists (WFTU) in 1946. However, in 1948, as the Cold War was heating up, the BTUC, in line with the British government’s policy of disabling the pro-worker and anti-colonial international union, claimed that the WFTU was Communist dominated and withdrew from it. 19 Rojas and O’Connor came under heavy pressure from British officials and unionists to also disaffiliate from the WFTU and join the pro-Western alternative, the International Confederation

However, they refused on the grounds that, since its inception in 1945, the WFTU had consistently supported colonial workers by advocating for them at the International Labour Office and the United Nations. They insisted that the accusations of Communist domination in the WFTU were simply a red herring meant to discredit the organization.20 When O’Connor, John La Rose, Cheedi Jagan, future Chief Minister of British Guiana, and some members of the Caribbean Labour Congress (CLC) travelled to Barbados hoping to negotiate with pro-ICTFU unionists, their gathering was erroneously portrayed as a “Communist plot to destroy the democratic government in the West Indies.” 21 In fact, as a result of his support of the WFTU and the CLC, O’Connor was put on a list of leftist unionists who were banned from travelling in the English speaking Caribbean. 22 When the issue of affiliation was put to a vote in Trinidad, the TTTUC agreed with O’Connor and Rojas’ position and voted in favour of staying affiliated with the WFTU, though six unions pulled out and aligned with the ICFTU. The Trinidad labour movement, as a consequence, was divided into rival federations and the TTTUC’s claim to representation of the whole labour movement was destroyed. In 1953 when the government threatened to cease dealing with the FWTU, O’Connor, as well as Rojas, finally conceded and disaffiliated from the WFTU, marking the defeat of Trinidad’s leftist labour leaders and the depoliticization of the Trinidadian labour movement.23

During his union career, O’Connor was also a member of the Caribbean Labour Congress (CLC), which was dedicated to organizing and assisting workers on a pan-Caribbean basis. As a member of the CLC, O’Connor exchanged information on labour conditions and wages throughout the British West Indies, shared his expertise with other West Indian unionists and, most notably, in 1951, effectively assisted in the representation of destitute workers at the Commission of Enquiry established to resolve the general strike in Antigua.24

Political Career

In the early 1930s, O’Connor associated with a small group of prominent Trinidadian leftist nationalists who gathered around the Portuguese-Trinidadian writer, Alfred Mendes. This informal group which came to include Gomes, CLR James, and RAC De Boissiere, among others, met to listen to music, discuss politics, and share their writing. During this time, Gomes published The Beacon, a literary magazine, which gave expression to the group’s social and political critique of Trinidad. In the early forties, O’Connor joined New Dawn, a group of middle class Marxists, who were in favour of Trinidadian independence. With its publication limited to circulation among labour and progressive circles, this group had a decidedly minor impact on the wider society. Also during the early forties, O’Connor ‘godfathered’ the Why Not Discussion Group, which was critical of British colonial policy and became a “focal point for dissent.”25

O’Connor entered the realm of electoral politics at the side of Albert Gomes. In the late thirties, O’Connor assisted Gomes get elected to the municipal government of Port of Spain before also being elected to the council a couple years later. In 1942, they both joined the West Indian National Party (WINP), which had been formed in the wake of the emergence of independence struggles across the colonial world and the Labour Party’s success in England during the early forties.26 The WINP had a radical programme, which called for self-government for a federated West Indies, immediate responsible government for Trinidad, and eventual nationalization of the oil, sugar, and asphalt industries. The party also proposed the redistribution of land, state sponsored public education and healthcare, and improved working conditions for all. The WINP’s programme quite unrealistically resembled the British Labour Party’s platform and, given Trinidad’s economic underdevelopment and colonial status at this time, can probably best be described as utopian socialist.27 Trinidad’s ethnic pluralism and geographically and ideologically divided labour movement were insurmountable obstacles to nationwide support for the WINP, which was unable to gain the support of East Indians or the unions based in the south.

Though the WINP as a party struggled to gain adherents, by the mid-40s, the Gomes- O’Connor partnership was proving politically fruitful. When Cipriani died in 1945 and his spot on the Legislative Council became available, Gomes with the support of O’Connor ran for and won the vacant seat. According to Gomes, “…no party machine propelled me to victory; but, with Quintin at my side, I just seemed to sail on a wave of popular enthusiasm to dizzy heights of electoral supremacy over my rival…”28

Early in 1946 with elections approaching, the WINP attempted to overcome the ethnic and labour divisions the party faced by joining the United Front (UF), the brain-child of leftist lawyer Jack Kelshall, who had hoped to avoid the splintering of the labour vote. 29 The UF initially consisted of the WINP, the Indian National Council (INC), the Oilfields Workers’ Trade Union (OWTU), and members of the NWCSA. However, the INC, feeling that the UF was too dominated by the WINP and too aligned with “Negro progressivism,” broke off. 30 Similarly, because of different views concerning the nationalization of the oil and sugar industries, as well as inter-union rivalries, the OWTU separated from the UF and formed the Trinidad and Tobago Trades Union Council and Socialist Party to run in the 1946 elections. 31 Despite the disunity of the United Front, Gomes, again with the campaign support of O’Connor, was successful in his bid to represent Port of Spain North in the Legislative Council, winning his seat with over 65% of the vote.32

Gomes would go on to become the most powerful local in Trinidad during the first half of the 1950s. However, by that time, O’Connor would view him as his main adversary rather than his political ally.33 Once Gomes was elected to the Legislative Council in 1946, the Governor General offered him a position on the Executive Council. 34 Gomes claims that he decided to accept the offer because he and O’Connor felt “that the time had come when a presence of our own at government level might exert a restraining influence on our behalf.” 35 However, shortly after he assumed his new position, he shed his left-wing politics and pro-union sympathies in favour of pro-capitalist trickle-down economics.36 Gomes came to believe “that instability in the ranks of labour could be the most damaging factor in the Island’s economy,” as it would scare away foreign investors, who he had hoped to attract with special privileges, such as income tax holidays.37 As early as December 1946, Gomes’s new allegiances were apparent as he supported the government when it ordered the invasion of the Seaman and Waterfront Workers’ Trade Union’s office and had the union’s leader arrested. Naturally, those on the left who had worked with and backed Gomes for upwards of fifteen years, especially O’Connor, felt betrayed by his shift to the right and came to see him as part of the Establishment.38

In the elections of 1950, O’Connor, no longer allied with Gomes or a member of the collapsed WINP, ran for a seat in the Legislative Council under the banner of the TUC. Receiving just over 30% of the vote, he lost in an upset to A.E. James of the TLP. In contrast, Gomes, having formed the Political Progress Group (PPG), which drew much of its support from the islands’ white minority and identified with the local business and professional elite, 39 won a seat on the Legislative Council and was again appointed to the Executive Council. 40 He was chosen as the chief minister and had the labour, industry, and commerce portfolio.

Undeterred by electoral defeat, O’Connor, along with others, founded the West Indian Independence Party (WIIP) in April of 1952. The party's founding programme called for an end to British imperialism, Trinidadian independence within a federated West Indies, the nationalization of the colony's major industries, and improvements in working conditions. In May of 1953, O'Connor, together with other members of the WIIP and the Trinidadian TUC held a demonstration to protest Gomes' economic policies, which, as O'Connor seemed to recognize at the time, ensured that profits from business done in Trinidad, instead of 'trickling down,' flowed out of the colony, and had little to no benefit for the island's working class and poor.41 Though the WIIP never obtained much popularity, it caught the attention of both London and the local authorities, who, given the climate of the Cold War, were worried about the spread of communism and/or recognized that they could use the communist bogey to tar their political

Rojas, O’Connor, and other old guard socialists founded the CNLP after failing to attain a satisfactory agreement with the leadership of the People’s National Movement (PNM). Besides personality issues between Rojas and Eric Williams, the PNM’s president and future Prime Minister of Trinidad, the CNLP was opposed to the sale of British oil interests to the American Texaco Oil Company, arguing that the Trinidad government should purchase the majority or a substantial portion of the shares of British oil companies operating in Trinidad, so as to avert American dominance in the oil industry.45 As a member of the CNLP, O’Connor ran again for a seat in the Legislative Council in the 1956 elections. He lost with just below 9% of the vote.46

Though O'Connor failed to win a seat on the Legislative Council, he was constantly active on behalf of a number of social and political causes. In 1946, he spoke in defense of panmen and their art when new restrictions were proposed and passed, which banned “the playing of noisy instruments on the streets and public places” without permission from the Governor or Police Commissioner.47 At a time when the majority of Trinidadians, especially the middle and upper classes, generally saw panmen as hooligans and their music as a nuisance, O'Connor was among the small group of middle class progressive nationalists who defended steelband, seeing in this indigenous art form the embryo of a national symbol. 48 O'Connor also spoke out when he heard reports of the racist treatment of black workers at the hands of White American bosses at the US Naval base in Chaguaramas. In the midst of World War II, he wrote to the Acting Colonial Secretary: "Negro haters are employed at the bases... men who believe Negroes are dogs to be brutally kicked, men who get drunk and want to beat up Negroes. We cannot have an ally fighting a war for freedom and committing acts worse than slave owners." 49 Also during the war, O’Connor successfully opposed the government’s attempt to pass a Sedition Act, which was intended to curtail civil liberties, such as the freedom of speech, freedom of assembly, and freedom of the press.50 When the government attempted to ban all public

When a Committee was established to consider the extension of the franchise, O'Connor submitted a memorandum on behalf of the FWTU arguing in favour of universal adult suffrage and warning that Trinidad would experience more "1937s" if workers remained excluded from the political system.53 Alluding to the fact that Trinidad had resorted to rationing in order to support the war effort, he wrote, "The people of this country have been encouraged to believe in democracy and no better time could be chosen to introduce them to the democratic way of life than when they are being asked to and they are making sacrifices to ensure the continued existence of democracy”54 O'Connor and other representatives of the left were also controversially in favour of a language test to ensure that all voters were literate, though this meant that the majority of East Indians in Trinidad would not be able to vote. According to political scientist John La Guerre, the left's insistence on a language test severely hurt its ability to appeal to Trinidadian East Indians, who were wary of attempts to assimilate them.55

In 1948, O'Connor was selected to sit on the Constitutional Reform Committee. Though he had been in favour of immediate responsible government during the 1946 elections, as a member of this committee he signed the majority report, which kept power ultimately in the hands of the Governor General. He later regretted this decision. At a CLC conference that same year, he agreed to withdraw his signature and supported a resolution in favour of Patrick Solomon’s minority report, which called for immediate self-government.56

Death and Legacy

Following O'Connor's sudden death from a stroke in 1958, he was given a hero's burial. A large procession followed his body to Lapeyrouse, where people delivered graveside panegyrics in his honour.57 Having witnessed how the people celebrated O’Connor’s life and work, CLR James, the Caribbean's most internationally known intellectual, felt that O'Connor should be recognized as an outstanding union leader and a great West Indian. It was at James’s behest that Lennox Pierre, O’Connor’s long-time comrade, published a short biography in order to keep the memory of O’Connor alive.58

Through his union activities, O’Connor gave practical expression to his “radical idealism” and made a valuable contribution to his society. In 1973, he was posthumously honoured by the Trinidadian government with the Chaconia Medal “For Long and Meritorious Service to Trinidad and Tobago” for his work in the birth and growth of the trade union movement.59 In 1985, the government also commemorated his accomplishments by including him in a series of stamps honouring great Trinidadian labour leaders. The Oilfields Workers' Trade Union’s library is named in honour of him.

When O’Connor first joined the Clerks’ Union of the TLP in the 1930s, there was virtually no labour movement to speak of, but, by the time of his death, unionism was an accepted institution of Trinidadian society. O’Connor played a critical role in this important transformation. In the face of the careerism that was rampant among his generation of union leaders, he demonstrated what it means to fulfill one's vocation. He was never paid much, and bequeathed little financially to the four children and wife he left behind. Though born into a family with the trappings of a middle class life, as an adult, he left most of these trappings behind and was always trying to improve the lot of others. His dogged representation of workers at bargaining tables throughout his career helped to normalize the process of collective bargaining. The concessions he won for government workers in 1946 linking wage increases to rises in the cost of living index was his small part in the struggle for a living wage, a struggle that continues for unions all over world even today. Despite what may be viewed as the futility of O’Connor’s attempts at establishing socialism in Trinidad, what he lacked in electoral success, he made up for in character. Through his sincerity, integrity, and passionate idealism, O’Connor gained the respect and admiration of his peers and even many of his political opponents. 60 As Jack Kelshall has reflected, “O’Connor’s mark on his generation is recognition of the fact that selfless and unswerving devotion to a cause is possible in a West Indian. It is recognition of the fact that at least one West Indian could hold on to his beliefs at a time when almost all of his fellows were conveniently revising or abandoning their faiths in the battle for ‘success.’” 61 O’Connor refused to become Machiavellian in his approach to politics at a time when Trinidadian politics was reputedly dominated by bobolists.62 In fact, this is at least part of the reason he was not successful in his political endeavours. In the Words of Others

Quintin O’Connor in the words of others...

O’Connor’s mark on his generation is recognition of the fact that selfless and unswerving devotion to a cause is possible in a West Indian. It is recognition of the fact that at least one West Indian could hold on to his beliefs at a time when almost all of his fellows were conveniently revising or abandoning their faiths in the battle for “success.” Jack Kelshall, Quintin O’Connor
He was simple; he was kindly; he was generous; he was selfless. His ready cheerfulness of spirit in childhood matured as he grew into a settled serenity of personality which told you he was fortunate in his marriage and happy in his work... Intellectual in the scientific mould, though a lover of poetry and music, he was painstaking, methodical, patient, persevering to the end... He had to be all these things to accomplish the task history assigned to him and he accepted of history. He was all that. Lennox Pierre, Quintin O’Connor
Fanatical and dedicated, Quintin lived for ‘the working class movement.’ The term is vague but it was not vague for him. It meant all the world’s oppressed. In his vision history was moving, inexorably, on the angry tide of the oppressed masses, to a lasting reconciliation of the differences that divide all men; and he saw it as every man’s duty to labour unremittingly to achieve this perfect state. And as he thought, so he lived. He resigned a lucrative job with good prospects to assist Captain Cipriani, and with the same casual disregard for personal
considerations withdrew when it seemed to him that more vigorous and determined protest had become necessary. A compelling mutuality brought us together... But we were as different as it is possible for two persons to be. I could never share his simple faith in human nature, a faith that is necessary to the vision of heaven on earth. Nor can I give myself as unstintingly to a cause as he did... Albert Gomes, Through a Maze of Colour
One of my best friends was Quintin O’Connor, leader of the Federated Workers’ Trade Union. With Albert Gomes, I visited his home for years every Sunday morning to discuss politics and the trade union movement in Trinidad. Quintin was a remarkable human being, a fanatic in his faith with a compassionate heart. Alfred Mendes, The Autobiography of Alfred H. Mendes, 1897–1991
Quintin was a man who never went back on his word. Patrick Solomon, Solomon: An Autobiography
... there was little room for parties, like the West Indian National Party (1942) and the Caribbean Socialist Party (1947), advocating reasoned ideological positions, nor for the occasional candidates imbued with real social ideals, like Quintin O’Connor or Dr. Patrick Solomon… The trade union field remains as anarchistically individualist as ever, and the typical trade union leader is not so much the radical idealist, like the late Quintin O’Connor... Gordon K. Lewis, The Growth of the Modern West Indies
[Ralph de Boissiere]’s rebellious sentiments drew him towards associating with such radical personalities as Quintin O’Connor, a great trade unionist, whose rectitude earned him the admiration even of his opponents, and from whom Ralph learned much… Michael Rogers Pocock, Out of the shadows of the past: the Story of the "Great House" of Champs Elysées...
[Eric Williams] does not possess—as did, say Quintin O’Connor in Trinidad or Roger Mais in Jamaica—a real love and affection for the common man… Quoted in Sealy’s Caribbean Leaders
...agitation of a mulatto named Quintin O’Connor, who is trying to organize base workers into his Federation of West Indian Laborers. He has at present no more than a hundred real members, but he is noisy and unscrupulous, and is dangerous because of his nuisance value. US intelligence report quoted in Base Colonies in the Western Hemisphere, 1940-1967
The Communists are weak in Trinidad. Their leader, Quintin O’Connor, is a real fanatic. He is very personable, but he is sincerely fanatical in his ideas, and would easily kill one opposed to him. Robert J. Alexander, Presidents, Prime ministers, and Governors of the English-speaking Caribbean and Puerto Rico: Conversations and Correspondence
